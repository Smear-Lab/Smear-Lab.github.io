We study active sensing: how animals move their bodies and sensory organs to gather information about the world. Our main system is olfaction, where sniffing is the sampling behavior and the olfactory bulb is the first place the brain processes what the nose picks up. We build closed-loop behavioral tools to study these processes by freely moving mice.

How we move shapes what we perceive, and what we perceive tells us how to move. That loop is the object of study. Breaking it apart, by head-fixing an animal or by presenting odors it did not seek, changes the thing you are measuring. So we let mice behave, and we measure the behavior as carefully as we measure the neurons.

The lab is run by Matt Smear in the Institute of Neuroscience and the Department of Psychology at the University of Oregon.

What we are asking

  • How does sniffing shape what the brain receives? Odor responses in the olfactory bulb are locked to the sniff with millisecond precision, so the animal's own sampling rhythm sets the clock the code is written against.
  • What else does the olfactory bulb track? In stimulus-free and task-free recordings, bulb population activity carries breathing state and the animal's position in space, and position decodes about as well from the bulb as it does from hippocampus under the same conditions.
  • How do animals search? Mice follow odor gradients with sniff-synchronized movements, and the search strategy is a behavior worth measuring in its own right, not a nuisance between trials.
  • How do we sample behavior itself well enough? Most assays record the same few actions in the same few places. Widening the behavioral sample before deciding what counts as signal is a methods problem we work on directly.
